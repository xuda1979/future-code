\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Long-horizon software agents accumulate execution history, and the
accumulation is not free: resubmitting it costs bytes that grow with
project length, and naively discarding it costs correctness. The
research question of this paper is the review's stronger form:

\begin{quote}\itshape
When can an agent stop remembering its execution history without
losing the current evidence and obligations needed to finish correctly?
\end{quote}

We answer it with a pre-registered mechanism study
(Section~\ref{sec:retire}). Ten context policies---from full replay
through observation masking, periodic summary/reset, bounded pages,
and a verified board, to verified-frontier retirement with typed
capsules---consume byte-identical seeded work streams with temporal
persistence, payload-bearing facts, persistent dependency footprints,
and obligations that come due later. Three closure rules were frozen
before data generation. Two fired against our own proposal, and the
manuscript is organized around the survivors.

The survivors are three. First, a falsification: the cheapest policy on
submitted bytes, periodic summary/reset, silently loses obligations
across reset boundaries and acts on stale values within windows,
failing the frozen acceptance contract on 42 of 63 runs; retention
policies that keep a typed pending set and current bindings hold
63/63. Stopping remembering is safe exactly when the discarded history
has been converted into (i)~current verified bindings for what the
work still touches, (ii)~a typed record of unresolved obligations, and
(iii)~interfaces to completed components the active work depends
on---and it is unsafe when any of those three survives only through a
lossy summary. Second, an operations result at equal correctness:
frontier retirement cuts on-demand retrieval operations by 48.3\%
versus observation masking, pre-registering the hypothesis that
live-agent savings, if any, come from avoided tool-call round trips
rather than bytes; the byte-level null closure triggered, and we claim
no byte-cost win over masking. Third, a conditional rule for the HACT
certificate-tree evidence layout: against a steel-manned flat ledger,
the learned tree wins on dense global updates ($-87.1\%$ held-out
bytes) and loses on sparse clustered and independent deltas
($+88.7\%$, $+49.2\%$), so it is demoted from the paper's center to a
density-selected component. The scheduler dimension is reported
separately and is byte-neutral by design.

This ordering is deliberate. The earlier versions of this work
(Sections~\ref{sec:model}--\ref{sec:eval}) optimized how verification
evidence is serialized under fixed test schedules; the review
correctly observed that this changes neither what an agent remembers
nor when it may forget. Those component results---the separation of
immutable check identities from mutable execution state, generation
fencing, the learned-order layouts, the codec- and service-matched
deployment test---are retained with their scope labels unchanged, now
in service of the retirement question: a capsule that carries verified
bindings and pending obligations needs an evidence layout when its
contents must cross a link, and the layout's value is conditional in
exactly the way the new ablation measures.

The negative controls remain: flat checker timings and per-episode
verification overhead are limits of the serialization claim; the
mechanism-level cohort is a limit of the retirement claim; the
live-agent protocol of Section~\ref{sec:live-protocol} has not been
run, and no hosted model, token count, or billing figure appears
anywhere in this paper as a measurement.
